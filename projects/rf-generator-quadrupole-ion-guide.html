RF Generator for Quadrupole Ion Guide · Spring 2026 to Present

A kilovolt RF generator for a mass spectrometer. I custom-wound a toroid transformer with 474% more gain at 1 MHz than a U-core, and now own the high voltage resonator board.

Electrical Engineering Consultant · Evan R. Williams Research Group, via Engineering Solutions at Berkeley · Feb 2026 to Present

The goal

A mass spectrometer weighs molecules by trapping them in fast switching electric fields. Those fields need a clean, high voltage signal, up to 1.5 kV swinging as fast as a million times a second. My part is the stage that builds that voltage.

What I did

Spring 2026: the custom transformer

Our team at the Williams Group is designing and constructing custom RF generators capable of producing 1 to 1.5 kV amplitude waveforms from 100 Hz to 10 MHz, able to drive capacitive loads up to 1 nF. The system has three components: a computer interface that synthesizes arbitrary waveforms and communicates with the host machine over SPI (requiring custom software drivers), a power amplifier built and tested from an existing design and modified for our application, and a center-tapped step-up transformer to produce two waveforms 180 degrees out of phase.

My work on this project was the transformer. I started by researching commercial transformers against our target specs (1:8 to 1:10 step-up ratio, 1.0 to 1.5 kV output, 100 Hz to 10 MHz frequency range, center-tapped for 180 degree outputs, capacitive load), and found that no existing part could satisfy all three of turns ratio, voltage, and frequency range simultaneously, so I moved to a custom-wound design. I selected toroidal cores over U-core geometries for lower leakage inductance, and chose Material 43 NiZn ferrite over MnZn for its insulating properties and suitability at RF frequencies. I calculated the minimum primary turn count needed to avoid core saturation at our lowest operating frequency, then wound and tested prototype transformers at a smaller turns ratio to validate the approach with a function generator swept across a wide frequency range.

The prototypes were a 22 mm Material 43 toroid and a Material 77 MnZn U-core, both wound at the same 20:80 turns ratio and swept from 50 kHz to 3 MHz. The toroid held a steady 2.9x to 3.9x step-up from 50 kHz to 1 MHz and stayed usable to about 1.5 MHz, with its self-resonance near 1.75 MHz spiking the gain to 10.5x. The U-core peaked near 360 kHz and then collapsed, delivering just 0.68x at 1 MHz, actually stepping the signal down. Both fell slightly short of the ideal 4x because the function generator's 50 ohm output impedance loaded the primary.

Fall 2026: the resonator board

After reviewing our spring results, the group scoped a follow-up phase with a broader set of loads. The generator now drives quadrupoles at up to 750 V amplitude on each of two antiphase outputs, riding on a 1 kV DC offset into about 300 pF, and/or ion funnels at 250 V peak to peak into 500 pF to 1 nF, anywhere from 10 kHz to 1 MHz. These loads are capacitors, so at high frequency they demand far more current than an amplifier can supply directly. The design gets around that with resonance.

The system is two boards. On the first, an STM32H7 takes commands over isolated USB and programs a DDS to generate a sine wave, which passes through a reconstruction filter and a variable gain amplifier into two PA107 power op amps driven in bridge. I own the second board, the resonator. It takes that drive through a step down transformer, sized to keep the amplifiers within their current limit, into a series LC tank. Relay-switched inductor sections from about 5 µH to 3.2 mH and a capacitor bank from 2 pF to 20 nF tune the tank to the requested frequency, so the rods' own capacitance resonates and builds up the high voltage. Amplitude and phase feedback go back to the first board to hold the output on resonance at the right level.

The architecture is settled at the block level, and I'm now designing the resonator PCB in KiCad, researching high voltage layout (creepage, clearance, isolation, and discharge safety), and running LTspice simulations of the tank. Cost has become a main constraint, so part of the work is weighing cheaper options like dual pole relays against their isolation limits.

Results

  • 474% higher step-up gain at 1 MHz than the U-core (3.9x vs 0.68x)
  • Resonator architecture settled; PCB design in progress
